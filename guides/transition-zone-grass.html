Choosing grass for the transition zone

Too hot for cool-season grass in July, too cold for most warm-season grass in January. Every choice is a compromise, so pick the one you can live with.

Where it is

A band across the middle of the country: roughly Kansas and Oklahoma east through Missouri, Kentucky, Tennessee and Virginia to the Carolinas. Think Kansas City, St. Louis, Louisville, Nashville, Washington DC and Raleigh.

How to choose

  • Trees and shade? Tall fescue. Bermuda will thin out under a tree canopy.
  • Full sun, kids, dogs, sports? Bermuda. It's the toughest and fastest to recover. Pick a cold-hardy variety (Tahoma 31, Latitude 36, Northbridge) in the northern half of the zone.
  • Sun to part shade, and you want low effort? Zoysia. It's dense, crowds out weeds, and many experts call it the best fit for the zone. Choose cold-hardy types (Meyer, Zeon, Zenith). The trade-offs: it's slow to establish, and it's brown half the year.
  • Must be green at Christmas? Tall fescue, or a warm-season lawn overseeded with ryegrass each fall (a yearly job, and it can slow spring green-up).
  • Northern edge or higher elevation? Kentucky bluegrass, alone or mixed with tall fescue, can work. Further south it struggles in summer.

Skip these in the transition zone

St. Augustine, centipede and bahia can die in a hard winter here. Leave them to the Gulf and the Deep South.

Switching grasses

Changing from fescue to a warm-season grass means killing the old lawn and starting over in late spring or early summer. Going the other way means seeding in fall. It's a big job, so be sure first. Often the fix is simply better care of the grass you already have.
